# Message gate

Nothing gets structured or written until these four are agreed: goal, message,
arguments, elephant. Propose; don't interrogate. Show a draft of all four and
let the user edit it.

## 1. Goal: a verb

A speech changes or decides something. Telling is not a goal. Write it as:

**After this, [who] will [do / decide / believe what] [by when].**

- Verbs that work: approve, fund, sign up, try, choose, change, remember.
- Not goals: "inform about", "present", "give an overview", "update on".
- Too big for one talk? Step one level down: not "get the €2M budget" but
  "get approval for a 6-week pilot".

## 2. Message: one line

The one sentence the audience must leave with. Lead with it. Say what is being
done and what it means, not everything that is known.

Propose 2–3 candidates. Run each through the four tests and show the result in
one line per candidate:

| Test | Passes if |
|---|---|
| **Retell** | An attendee could repeat it, almost word for word, to a colleague who missed the talk |
| **Headline** | A journalist could print it as the headline you'd want |
| **Banner** | It works as a slogan on a supporter's sign |
| **3 seconds** | It fits in one breath (about 10 words or fewer) |

A message that fails two or more tests is a topic, not a message. Rewrite it.

Backup from well-known frameworks:

- TED's **throughline** (Chris Anderson): the idea that connects every part, in 15 words or fewer.
- **Answer first** (Barbara Minto): when the audience asks "what's going on?", lead
  with the conclusion and the action, then the facts.
- **Made to Stick** (Chip and Dan Heath): simple, unexpected, concrete, credible,
  emotional, story.

## 3. Arguments: three at most

People don't remember more than three, and neither does the speaker. Even for a
broad topic, pick the three that best support the goal. For each one:

- **Island, not plane.** Say what the listener gets (the island), not how you
  work or what you built (the plane). Keep asking "so what, for them?" until the
  answer is about them.
- **Argument, not means.** "Competitors already ship weekly" is an argument; "we
  use Kubernetes" is a means. Technical detail goes to Q&A or the appendix.
- **Proof.** Attach one piece of evidence: a number, an example, a demo, a story.
  If the user hasn't given it, write `[NEEDS SOURCE: …]`.
- **Tag.** Give it a 2–3 word tag. The closing repeats the tags.

Sharpen the problem: the audience must feel it before they want the solution.
Arguments can sit inside the problem or the solution. Urgency often makes a
strong third argument: delay isn't neutral, it has a cost.

## 4. Audience and the elephant

First decide what you want, then meet the audience where they are: what they
know, how they feel about the topic, what they gain or lose.

Ask: **why would they not do what I ask?** The answer is the elephant in the room.

| Elephant | Signs | Park it by |
|---|---|---|
| Fear | risk, blame, the unknown | showing a small first step and the safety net |
| Distrust | of you, your team, past promises | naming it: "I know the last rollout didn't go well, so…" |
| Loss of power or status | their area is affected | showing what they gain or keep |
| Habit / tradition | "we've always done it this way" | honouring the past, then showing what changed |
| Beliefs, values | a clash with what they hold | starting from a shared value |
| Inattention | tired, busy, last slot | a stronger hook, a shorter talk |
| (In)experience | too expert or too new | adjusting the level; the neighbour test |

Lack of information is rarely the elephant: people often know and still don't
act (everyone knows exercise is good). You don't have to chase the elephant out
of the room. Name it and deal with it ("park" it): in the opening, in an
argument, or in a prepared Q&A answer.

## Gate passed when

- [ ] The goal is a verb, with a who and a when
- [ ] The message passes at least 3 of the 4 tests
- [ ] At most 3 arguments, each with listener value, proof (or `[NEEDS SOURCE]`) and a tag
- [ ] The elephant is named, with a plan to park it
- [ ] The user has confirmed

If the user explicitly says not to ask ("just write it"), pick the strongest
candidate, put the message and goal at the top of `brief.md` marked
**unconfirmed**, and continue.
