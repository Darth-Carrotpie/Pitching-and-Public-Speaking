# Script

The script is the full spoken text. Write it to be heard, not read.

## Format

~~~markdown
# <Title>: script
N words = A s · P long pauses = B s · total T s · limit L s (90% of X min)

## Opening (0:00–0:30) [SLIDE 1]
[MEMORIZE] First sentence. / Second sentence. // ...

## Argument 1: <tag> (0:30–1:20) [SLIDE 2]
...
~~~

Cues: `/` short pause · `//` long pause (2–3 s) · **bold** = stress ·
`[SLIDE n]` = advance · `[MEMORIZE]` = learn word for word · `[NEEDS SOURCE: …]`.
Give every section a timestamp and put the timing sum at the top (see "Timing").

## When the user said "just write it"

The main script must work aloud as it is, built only from the facts given: no
essential blanks. Where a personal detail would make it better, write a complete
line that needs no invented facts, and offer the personal version separately as
`Optional insert: …`. Use a placeholder only for a fact the speech can't work
without (a date, a sign-up link), and keep those few.

## Opening (about 15%)

The first sentence creates curiosity. Write **3 alternative openings**, each
from a different family, and recommend one:

| Family | Moves |
|---|---|
| **Connect** | a shared experience, a short personal story, self-deprecation |
| **Engage** | a question, a story, a vivid image, a small task for the audience, humour (only if natural to the speaker) |
| **Surprise** | a quote, a statistic, a strong or exaggerated claim, the worst-case scenario, a fact the audience doesn't know |

- At most 2 questions in the opening.
- Don't open with "Hello, my name is…, thank you for having me". If the name
  matters, put it inside the story.
- Be different from what the other speakers on the day will do.
- Mark the first 3–5 sentences `[MEMORIZE]`.
- End the opening on the message, or on the promise of the talk.

## Body

For each argument: **claim → proof → what it means for you** (the listener).

- Words: short, familiar, conversational. Sentences: one idea each, mostly under 15 words.
- Pause after key lines. People remember what comes just before a silence.
- Every abstract claim gets a concrete example, illustration or comparison.
- Signpost: "First…", "That's the problem. Here's what we do about it."
- Ask the audience questions where it fits. Don't only make statements.

### Amplifiers: use each one at least once

- **Image.** A picture the audience can see in their heads, or on a slide. Images
  are understood faster and remembered longer.
- **Emotion.** A moment people feel. Reach past sight and sound: touch, smell,
  taste, pain, pleasure. The more senses, the stronger it lands.
- **Rhythm.** Repetition, alliteration, triads, anaphora ("I have a dream… I have
  a dream…"), sound echoes ("the race to replace").

Analogies are powerful and risky. Flag each one for the outsider test in the rehearsal kit.

## Closing (the most important part)

The last impression is the one that lasts. Protect its time: if time runs short,
cut body content, never the closing.

1. **Summary.** Repeat the arguments, each shrunk to its 2–3 word tag. Some
   people drifted off; they need this.
2. **Call to action.** More concrete than the message: what do listeners do
   when they leave the room? What first step starts the change? Who, what, by when?
3. **Your why** (optional). When people must be strongly motivated: why this
   matters to you personally, in a short story.
4. **Last line.** Echo the opening image or phrase if you can. Then stop.

- Never end on "thank you", "that's all" or "any questions?". Stay on the same
  level as the audience; your last words are the CTA.
- If there's Q&A, write a 20–30 second **final close** for after the questions:
  the message and the CTA once more.

## Clarity filters ("ŽABT")

Run the finished draft through these:

- **Person.** Write to one specific non-expert: a neighbour, a grandmother.
  Would they follow? Picturing one person instead of a crowd gives you spoken
  language instead of written.
- **Active.** Verbs over nouns. First person: "it is believed" → "I think".
  Owning the words shows you take responsibility for them.
- **Simple.** Swap complex or professional words for everyday ones. Jargon
  distances the audience; plain speech signals "one of us".
- **Short.** Cut the text in half, then in half again. Shorten sentences. Delete
  words that add nothing.

Nobody cares how much work went in. Present the value.

## Numbers

Round them and make them concrete: "1 in 3 customers", not "33.4%". One number
per sentence. Compare it to something familiar. Every number needs a source
from the user, or `[NEEDS SOURCE]`.

## Timing

Count, don't estimate:

1. **Words**: only the spoken text of the main version. Skip headings, cues, stage
   directions, alternative openings and placeholder instructions. Count each
   placeholder at its stated budget (`[story, 30–40 words]` = 40).
2. **Seconds** = words ÷ 130 × 60 **+ 2.5 s per `//`** + every timed moment
   (a show of hands, raising glasses, applause, a demo).
3. **Limit** = 0.9 × slot in seconds. Seconds must be ≤ the limit.

Show the sum in the header, e.g. `190 words = 88 s · 8 long pauses = 20 s · total
108 s · limit 108 s`. Recompute after every cut, optional part or swapped opening.
In short speeches, long pauses are expensive: keep only the ones that matter.
Shorter than the limit is fine if the goal is met. (130 wpm is a default; if
the language or speaker is slower, say so and adjust.)
