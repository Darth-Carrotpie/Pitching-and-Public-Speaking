## Practice plan

1. Read the script aloud once. Keep the run between 4:15 and 4:30; simplify any phrase that causes a stumble.
2. Memorize the opening through “soft extra” and the entire closing. Practise entering both without looking down.
3. Deliver three runs from the cue cards. On one run, pause for three seconds after every full stop to stop yourself rushing.
4. Record a final standing run with the slides. Check eye contact, filler words, slide changes and whether the five-second QR silence feels intentional.
5. Dress-rehearse the QR scan on the presentation screen and leave Slide 6 displayed after the last line.

## Three techniques to focus on

1. **Pause for the contrast.** Leave space after “This is wrong,” after the rewritten comment and before the CTA. The contrast carries the argument.
2. **Lighthouse eye contact.** Give one complete thought to one person, then move to another part of the room. Look at the audience—not the diagram—when naming each pattern.
3. **Move with purpose.** Stay still for the examples. At “So here is the next step,” move once toward the screen, indicate the QR, then stop speaking for five seconds.

## Outsider test

Test the two analogies—“a feedback conversation in disguise” and “a shared debugging session”—on someone outside software. Ask them to retell the central message. For this developer audience, keep both only if the phrasing still feels natural in rehearsal.

## Likely questions

Answers are designed for informal questions after the timed talk.

| Question | Short answer and bridge | Have ready |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Isn’t this just common sense?** | The idea may sound obvious. The useful test is what happens under pressure: does the comment reveal the concern and offer a next step? That is a developer skill worth practising. | The pull-request contrast |
| **2. Isn’t code review about code, not feelings?** | Specificity is not softness. Name the technical concern, explain its effect and avoid making the person the problem. That produces clearer technical feedback. | Slide 3 pattern |
| **3. Won’t this slow reviews down?** | A clear comment may take more words. I am not making a speed claim; the immediate value is that the receiver knows what concern to examine next. | No performance claim or figure |
| **4. What if we still disagree?** | Restate the concern, name the trade-off and clarify who owns the decision. Good feedback does not require agreement; it makes the disagreement usable. | Backup Slide B concept |
| **5. What if my feedback is ignored?** | Check that the concern and impact were understood. Ask for the other person’s reasoning, agree the decision owner and use the team’s escalation path if a real risk remains. | Team-specific process, if asked |
| **6. How can a junior developer challenge a senior?** | Start with an observation and a genuine question: “I noticed this path has no limit—what protects it here?” That invites context without hiding the concern. | A relevant technical example |
| **7. Does this work across cultures or in remote teams?** | No single script fits every person or channel. Ask about preferences and adapt the wording, while keeping the concern specific and actionable. | Team norms, if available |
| **8. Can a workshop guarantee better teams?** | No workshop can guarantee that. The workshop is a first step; the message today is that feedback is a skill developers must keep practising. | Hardest-question answer |
| **9. What happens at the workshop, and how do I join?** | State only the confirmed facts: it is free and on 12 November. Then point to the published signup details. | [NEEDS SOURCE: signup URL, time, venue or online status, agenda, facilitator and accessibility details] |
