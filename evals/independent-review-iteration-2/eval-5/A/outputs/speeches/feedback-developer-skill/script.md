## Argument 1: Clear reasoning (0:37–1:23) [SLIDE 2]

First: clarity.

Every pull request is a feedback conversation in disguise. When you name a confusing function, ask for a test, or explain a trade-off, you are helping another developer see what you see.

“This is wrong” hides the useful part: your reasoning. /

Try this instead: “I’m worried this retry has no limit. If the service stays down, could this loop forever? What do you think about adding a cap?” /

Same concern. Completely different experience.

The receiver gets a risk, a reason and a route forward. **Clear feedback replaces guessing with a next step.** //

## Argument 2: Shared problem (1:23–2:22) [SLIDE 3]

Second: make it a shared problem.

Giving feedback well does not mean being endlessly nice. It means being specific enough to be useful, and human enough to be heard. /

You can do that with a simple pattern. Say what you noticed. Explain the effect. Invite the next step.

Notice: “I had to jump between several files to follow this request.”

Effect: “That makes the flow hard for me to verify.”

Next step: “Could we keep it together, or add a short map?” /

No verdict about the person. No vague order. Just something both developers can examine.

[SKIP IF LATE] Use the same habit in pairing, design debates, retrospectives and one-to-ones. You can challenge the work without turning the challenge into a judgment on the person. //

## Argument 3: Deliberate practice (2:22–3:18) [SLIDE 4]

Third: practise, do not just agree.

Most of us already believe feedback matters. That belief will not rescue us when a tense sentence appears in a chat box and the cursor starts blinking. /

When you ask for feedback, replace “Thoughts?” with a target: “Where could this design fail under load?” When you give it, replace labels with observations. When you receive it, ask, “What did you notice?” before you defend your intent. /

Small changes in language give the conversation somewhere useful to go.

And that is why this deserves practice, not just good intentions.

Practice makes that intention concrete: the next time the cursor blinks, pause, name what you noticed, and offer a useful next step. //

## Visualization (3:18–3:44) [SLIDE 5]

Imagine your next review. The concern is clear. The author understands why it matters. They may still disagree—and that is fine. Now you are discussing the trade-off, not decoding the tone. /

The review becomes a shared debugging session: not you versus me, but both of us versus the problem. //
