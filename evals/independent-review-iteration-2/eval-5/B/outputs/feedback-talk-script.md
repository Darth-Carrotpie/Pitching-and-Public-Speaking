## Slide 1 — Feedback is a developer skill (0:00–0:40)

Quick show of hands: who has received a code-review comment that was technically correct and still completely useless?

Maybe it just said, “This is messy.”

[Pause for hands or reactions.]

Now, who has written a comment like that on a Friday afternoon?

[Raise your own hand.]

Me too. The problem usually isn’t intelligence or intent. We learned syntax, testing and architecture, but many of us were never taught how to make feedback usable.

## Slide 2 — Feedback is already in your job (0:40–1:20)

Yet feedback is not an optional “people skill” sitting beside development. It is part of development.

Every pull request asks, “What should change?” Pairing requires us to challenge an idea without shutting down the person who offered it. Stand-ups and retrospectives depend on people surfacing problems early.

We work through technical interfaces all day. Feedback is the interface between the humans doing the work—and the quality of that interface affects everything downstream.

## Slide 3 — Bad feedback creates hidden work (1:20–2:05)

When feedback is vague, the other person has to guess what we mean. When it sounds personal, they spend energy defending themselves instead of examining the work. When it arrives late, a small correction becomes rework. When we avoid it completely, the same problem returns.

That is why this matters to every developer, not only managers. Giving feedback well reduces friction around the work we already have to do. It helps useful information travel while there is still time to act on it.

## Slide 4 — Send three fields, not a verdict (2:05–3:15)

Here is one pattern you can use today. Think of useful feedback as a small packet with three fields.

First: **observation**. Say what you actually saw or heard, without guessing at motive.

Second: **impact**. Explain why it matters—to the user, the code, the team or the delivery.

Third: **next step**. Suggest a change or ask a question that opens a conversation.

Observation, impact, next step.

This keeps feedback about something the person can examine and act on. It also gives them room to add context you may not have. You are sending a useful signal, not handing down a verdict.

For a sensitive conversation, add two basics: speak privately, and check whether now is a workable time.

## Slide 5 — Same concern, better signal (3:15–4:10)

So instead of writing, “This is over-engineered,” try this:

“This helper adds three layers around one API call. That makes failures harder to trace. Could we inline it until we have a second caller?”

Notice what changed. The concern is still direct. We did not dilute it or add a compliment sandwich. But now the author can see the evidence, understand the consequence and respond to a concrete proposal.

Good feedback is not about being endlessly nice. It is about making the truth easier to use.
