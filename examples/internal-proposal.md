# Example: 3-minute proposal to leadership

> Fictional company. Shows answer-first structure, an elephant parked up front, and a three-part ask.

## The request

> I have 3 minutes at Monday's leadership meeting (the COO and 5 department heads). I want to replace
> our weekly 90-minute status meeting (12 people) with written async updates. Two teams already tried it
> informally for a month and liked it. I'd like approval for a proper pilot. A few slides are fine.

## Intake

> 1. **Goal**: what exactly should they approve? *Suggested: a 6-week pilot with two teams, starting next Monday.*
> 2. **Audience**: who decides, and how do they feel about it? *Suggested: the COO decides; department heads
>    worry they'll lose visibility.*
> 3. **Material**: anything measured during the informal month? *Suggested: none, so no numbers beyond the
>    meeting arithmetic.*

User: "Yes, yes, and right, we didn't measure anything."

## Message gate

**Goal:** On Monday, the COO approves a 6-week async-update pilot with two teams, starting the following Monday.

**Message (confirmed):** Same visibility, 18 hours a week back. Let's test it for 6 weeks.
(Retell ✓ · Headline ✓ · Banner ✓ · 3 s ✓)

The 18 hours is arithmetic from the user's own facts: 12 people × 90 minutes.

**Arguments**
1. **Hours back.** 18 person-hours every week return to real work.
2. **Better visibility.** Written updates are searchable; nobody has to wait for Thursday to know.
3. **Safe to try.** 6 weeks, two teams, and an easy way back.

**Elephant:** loss of control. "If we don't meet, I won't know what's going on."
Parked in the opening (named directly) and in argument 2.

## Structure options offered

1. **Answer first / SCQA** (recommended): leaders want the recommendation first.
2. **Proof**: tell → justify → invite. Equally short, but the elephant comes later.
3. **Situation**: situation → problem → solution. Reads like a history lesson for this audience.

User picked 1.

## Output: `speeches/async-pilot-leadership/`

### script.md

~~~markdown
# Async updates pilot: script
~185 words · ~1:25 at 130 wpm · slot: 3 min

## Answer (0:00–0:12) [SLIDE 1]
[MEMORIZE] I'm asking for one decision today: /
a six-week pilot / where two teams replace the Thursday status meeting / with written updates. //
Same visibility. / Eighteen hours a week back.

## Situation and complication (0:12–0:26)
Every Thursday, twelve of us spend ninety minutes / hearing updates we could read in five. //
That's eighteen hours a week. / Almost half a person, / every week of the year.

## The elephant (0:26–0:38) [SLIDE 2]
I know the worry. / I'd have it too. / "If we stop meeting, / how will I know what's going on?" //
That's exactly the point I want to test.

## Arguments (0:38–1:05)
First, **hours back**. / Eighteen hours return to real work. /
Second, **better visibility**. / A written update is there on Monday morning, / not on Thursday afternoon. /
You can search it. / You can forward it. / You don't have to remember it. /
Third, **safe to try**. / Six weeks. / Two teams. / If visibility drops, / we bring the meeting back on day one.

## Ask and close (1:05–1:25) [SLIDE 3]
So I'm asking for three things: /
your approval today, / two volunteer teams by Friday, / and a fifteen-minute review with you in six weeks. //
Hours back. / Better visibility. / Safe to try. //
Let's find out on Thursday afternoons / what we do with eighteen extra hours.
~~~

### rehearsal.md (excerpt)

**Q&A prep (3 of 8)**
- *"How do we know it worked?"*: "Two measures: decisions delayed waiting for the meeting, and how many updates
  are read on time. We'll set the baseline in week one." (No numbers claimed from the informal month.)
- *"What if people don't write the updates?"*: "Then the pilot tells us something important, early. Team leads
  own the update, like they own the meeting today."
- *"Why not just make the meeting shorter?"*: "That saves time, but not the waiting. Written updates fix both."

> **Needs you (0):** no sources missing. The only number is 12 × 90 minutes.
